## Things That Are Delightful About The Floating Press

- Placing a stamp on the paper and watching the ink take hold.
- The way the indigo pools against the ochre.
- Choosing a single stamp and pressing it with love, rather than filling the page with clutter.
- The print overlay at the end — that moment when you see your creation rendered large and crisp, like a real woodblock pulled from the press.
- The secret satisfaction of removing a layer you don't like and watching the composition improve.

## How It Works

This is a print studio, not a painting app. You do not paint freely. You compose, like an ukiyo-e artisan choosing which blocks to run through the press.

**Choose a stamp.** A wave, a petal, a crane, a mountain, bamboo, cloud, a koi, a fan. Each is drawn from the motifs of Edo: bold lines, simple shapes, unmistakable silhouette.

**Choose an ink.** Eight colors from the traditional woodblock palette — indigo, vermilion, ochre, sage, ebicha (the deep plum), sumi (the pure black), beni (the precious red), and mizu (the water blue).

**Place it.** Tap the canvas. The stamp appears where your finger lands. You may add as many as you like, though a good print rarely needs more than a few.

**Rearrange.** Layers can be removed, the composition refined. A floating world is always in flux.

**Print.** When you are satisfied, press Print. The composition renders full-resolution, appears against a dark overlay, and you may admire it before it vanishes — for like all things in the floating world, it was never meant to last forever.

## On Ephemerality

Tsutaya asked me this morning: "Why not let them save the print?"

Because that is not the point, I told him. A woodblock print in Edo cost the price of a bowl of noodles. You bought it, enjoyed it for a season, and it yellowed. You replaced it with the next one.

The Floating Press respects this. You may make a print, admire it, and close the overlay. It is gone. The paper is blank again. You start a new one.

This is not a loss. This is the nature of the floating world.

## What Is Building

Hiroshige made the atmosphere — the timing of the stamps, the way they settle on the paper, the weight of the brush.

Utamaro designed the palette and the stamps. His eye for color is uncanny: he chose exactly eight, no more, because he said "more colors tempt the user to make clutter, not art."

Hokusai sketched the stamp shapes in a fury at 3 AM and declared them perfect, then said they were worthless and started again. We used the first set.

I named the thing and wrote this.

Come make a print. It will be brief. It will be beautiful. That is all it needs to be.

— Sei Shonagon, in her Pillow Book, noting that the best things are always the shortest.
